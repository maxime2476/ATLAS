\section{Further checks on maize}
\label[appendix]{app:maize-checks}

This appendix gives the details of two groups of checks summarised in \cref{sec:maize}. All were added after the main estimates and are descriptive.

\subsection{Heterogeneity of the slope}

The interaction of heat with the irrigated share of grain maize is not detectable (two-sided $p = \IrrInterP$). Department-specific slopes can be shrunk towards their mean with an empirical Bayes estimator \parencite{dersimonian1986}, but their estimates are not independent: they come from one regression with common year effects, and heat is correlated across departments, so with a covariance clustered by year their median correlation is \SlopeCorMed. Treating them as independent, as I first did, the slopes show little dispersion ($\tau = \EbTauDiag$; homogeneity test $p = \EbQpDiag$). With their full covariance, $\tau = \EbTau$, the common mean moves to \EbMu, above the pooled estimate, and the usual $\chi^2$ test of homogeneity rejects strongly ($p \EbQpRel$). This covariance is nearly singular, and treating it as known makes these quantities unreliable: $\tau$ depends on the covariance chosen and is not identified with these data, and some shrunk slopes move away from the common mean instead of towards it. That covariance is estimated from 26 years, and a test on 16 restrictions with so few clusters can reject far too often: on the placebos where heat is taken from other years, it rejects in \HomogChiSize\,\% of cases. Calibrated instead by the restricted wild bootstrap by year (same statistic, placebo size \HomogSize\,\%), the test gives $p = \HomogP$: by its criterion, heterogeneity of the slopes is \HomogReading. The shrunk slopes with the full covariance are therefore not interpreted: they lie between \EbMin{} and \EbMax, and their correlations with the irrigated share ($\EbCorIrr$) and with the altitude gap ($\EbCorAlt$, on 17 points) are given for completeness only.

\subsection{Composition of the land}

First, one more degree-day above 32\,$^{\circ}$C is associated with a change of $\CompEst$ in the irrigated share of grain maize area ($p = \CompP$, clustered by year, for information only). This check has little power by construction: areas are largely decided at sowing, before the summer heat. Second, and closer to the more plausible threat, rainfed maize sown for grain may be harvested as silage in dry summers. Over 2010--2025, the only years for which the SAA reports silage maize separately by irrigation mode, the share of rainfed maize harvested as silage (\SilageShare\,\% on average) does not rise detectably in hotter summers (one-sided $p = \SilageP$; added later). This check misses 2003, and silage areas may be recorded before the harvest decision is final. Abandoned fields, finally, stay in the statistics with a zero yield.
