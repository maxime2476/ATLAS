\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

This section asks three questions. What does the maize result mean, and what could make it wrong? How far does it travel beyond the 17 departments studied? And what did the way the project was run teach, independently of its results?

\subsection{What the maize result means}

In the main sample, irrigated maize keeps about \IrrShareOfRainfedSens\,\% of the heat sensitivity of rainfed maize: about $-$\QoneIrrigatedPct\,\% per degree-day above 32\,$^{\circ}$C against $-$\QoneRainfedPct\,\%, a ratio of two imprecise coefficients, given without an interval. The direction matches studies abroad, which find a much smaller heat sensitivity for irrigated than for rainfed maize in the United States \parencite{schauberger2017} and, for wheat in India, about a quarter of the fully rainfed sensitivity \parencite{zaveri2019}. The data, crops and designs differ too much for a closer comparison of magnitudes.

Two features of the result deserve emphasis. Irrigation is associated with a smaller sensitivity to heat, not with its disappearance: irrigated maize still loses yield in hot summers. And the association rests largely on a few summers, above all 2003, which carries between a quarter and two fifths of the useful variation in heat, depending on whether weights and soil moisture are taken into account (\cref{sec:exposure,subsec:maize-inference}). This is why the strength of the evidence depends on the inference: the bootstrap by year rejects, the cluster jackknife does not when read against a Student distribution, and does when its statistic is bootstrapped. A linear effect per degree-day is a convenient summary, not a law: in 2003 the observed widening of the gap was smaller than the linear estimate predicts (\cref{sec:maize}).

\paragraph{Heat, or hot and dry summers.} In France the summers with many degree-days above 32\,$^{\circ}$C (2003, 2019, 2022) were also dry: in the main sample, their July--August soil wetness index was \SwiTwoThousandThree, \SwiTwentyNineteen{} and \SwiTwentyTwentyTwo, against \SwiMean{} on average, and all three are among the \SwiRankMax{} driest summers since 2000. The soil wetness index absorbs part of this, but it comes from a land-surface model whose documentation does not mention irrigation, and the result holds when it is dropped or replaced by a climatic water balance (\cref{sec:maize}), but heat and the water balance remain correlated. ``Heat'' in this monograph therefore means hot, and mostly dry, summers. The vapour pressure deficit makes this precise: in a model with both, the association moves from degree-days above 32\,$^{\circ}$C to the drying power of the air (\cref{subsec:maize-vpd}), as \textcite{lobell2013} found for American maize, although the same split is not found for silage maize. The two are too correlated to be separated here, and the deficit, computed from the daily maximum temperature, also tracks average heat; the safest reading of the main result is that the advantage of irrigated maize is larger in hot, dry summers, more than that it is larger in summers with extreme heat as such. Declared irrigation withdrawals point the same way: they rise in summers with dry soils, not detectably with degree-days (\cref{subsec:maize-vpd}). Whether the association reflects the water supplied or also a cooler canopy on irrigated fields cannot be told apart with these data. Satellite studies elsewhere attribute about a third of the benefit of irrigation to cooling \parencite{zhu2022}, and heat stress computed from air temperature overstates the stress of irrigated crops \parencite{siebert2017}; with air temperature common to both modes, as here, part of what is called heat may be the absence of this cooling on rainfed fields. A check with MODIS surface temperature is consistent with this reading but cannot settle it, since that temperature is averaged over 8~km cells in which maize covers a small share of the land and also rises with the water stress of the crop: across the 38 departments, surface temperature rises by about \LstSlope{}\,$^{\circ}$C per degree-day above 32\,$^{\circ}$C, a little less where more maize is irrigated (\LstSlopeQone{} at the first quartile of the irrigated share, \LstSlopeQthree{} at the third; two-sided $p = \LstInterP$), but the irrigated share also differs across departments in other ways.

\paragraph{Water restrictions.} The planned interaction with crisis-level restrictions has the opposite sign to the hypothesis (\cref{sec:maize}). It is read descriptively, as planned, and it gives no support to the idea that restrictions reduced the advantage of irrigated maize in these departments.

\subsection{What could make it wrong}
\label{subsec:discussion-wrong}

The estimate is an association within departments and years. For it to measure how irrigation changes the response to heat, irrigated and rainfed land would have to differ only in ways that do not themselves vary with the heat of the summer. Several mechanisms could break this.

\paragraph{Silage.} In a hot, dry summer, some rainfed maize sown for grain may be harvested as silage instead; in United States counties, the harvested share of planted maize and soybeans falls above 35\,$^{\circ}$C, less so for maize \parencite{cui2020}. Abandonment is less of a concern: the SAA counts unharvested area with a zero yield, so abandoned fields stay in the grain statistics. Silage is different. If the worst rainfed fields move from grain to silage, the observed rainfed grain yield is the yield of the remaining fields and overstates what rainfed maize produced. Under that assumption, this would bias the gap downwards in hot years, against the result; if the fields moved to silage are not the worst ones, the direction of the bias is not known. The SAA areas of silage maize, available for 2010--2025, show no detectable increase in the share of rainfed maize harvested as silage in hotter summers (\cref{subsec:maize-composition}), but 2003 is outside that period and the areas may be recorded before harvest. The irrigated share of grain maize area does not move detectably with heat (\cref{sec:maize}), but areas are largely decided at sowing, so this check cannot see the mechanism.

\paragraph{Irrigation itself responds to the summer.} Farmers irrigate more in dry summers, unless restrictions stop them: declared withdrawals rise with soil dryness, though not detectably with degree-days at a given soil wetness (\cref{subsec:maize-vpd}). The estimate therefore mixes the agronomic effect of water with the behavioural response of irrigators, two effects that reduced-form weather models do not separate \parencite{bareille2024}. This is part of what ``irrigated maize'' means in practice, and it is why the result describes the observed advantage of irrigated maize, not the effect of a fixed amount of water.

\paragraph{Other changes in the departments.} The year effects absorb national trends, not changes specific to a department that would move with its summers, such as a shift in varieties or in irrigation equipment; these are not observed here.

\paragraph{Different land, different farms.} Irrigated fields tend to be on soils and farms suited to intensive maize. Department fixed effects absorb the stable part of these differences. They would not absorb, for example, a tendency of better farms to protect their irrigated maize more in hot years.

\paragraph{Measurement.} Four sources of error are documented in the monograph:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the result depends on measuring heat where maize grows (\cref{sec:maize});
  \item SIM2 is an 8~km reanalysis; E-OBS, interpolated from stations, counts more degree-days above 32\,$^{\circ}$C, and the result holds with both;
  \item the masks do not distinguish irrigated from rainfed parcels, so both modes are assigned the same heat;
  \item fixed calendar windows ignore year-to-year shifts in crop development \parencite{leroux2024}.
\end{itemize}
Classical errors in heat would bias $\beta$ towards zero, and instrumenting the SIM2 measure by E-OBS raises the estimate by about a third (\cref{subsec:maize-measure}); but errors in a threshold variable are not classical, errors that differ systematically between irrigated and rainfed land have no known direction, and other checks give smaller estimates, so the net direction of the bias is not known.

\paragraph{The data on yields.} The quality status of 2025 is applied to 2000--2009, two releases of the statistics are spliced in 2010, many yields are rounded, and the 2025 values are provisional. The splice was checked on the overlap and the results do not depend on 2025, but the older decade rests on a release that was never revised.

\paragraph{How the split between irrigated and rainfed maize is produced.} The SAA is a synthesis of several sources, compiled by regional statistical services. This monograph does not document how they set the irrigated and rainfed yields separately, nor whether ``irrigated'' means land actually irrigated that year or land equipped for irrigation. If part of the split were estimated from expert judgement about dry summers, the gap could reflect the estimation method as much as the harvest. This was flagged when the project was scoped, and a check added later finds a trace of it. In the main sample, the ratio of irrigated to rainfed yield, rounded to the hundredth, is the same as in the previous year in \SaaRepPct\,\% of cases, against \SaaRepRefPct\,\% when rainfed years are shuffled within each department (95th percentile \SaaRepPnf\,\%). Of these \SaaRepTot{} repetitions, \SaaRepOld{} fall in the older release used for 2000--2009, and \SaaRepTwoThousandThree{} in 2003 itself. Rounded ratios are not more frequent than chance ($p = \SaaRoundP$), the splice of 2010 shows no break, and repetitions are not more frequent in hot summers ($p = \SaaHeatP$). A share of the older split thus seems to have been carried over from one year to the next, as an imputation would do. Dropping the \SaaRepTot{} department-years concerned leaves the estimate at \NoRepeatPct\,\% per degree-day ($p = \NoRepeatP$, \NoRepeatN{} department-years; added later). An imputation of that kind would keep the gap at its previous level, which works against finding a response to heat, but this direction is not tested. The criterion of this check then dropped the \SaaSixDeps{} departments with at least a quarter of their years flagged (most of them by rounded ratios, which on their own are not unusual): without them, the estimate is \SaaSixPct\,\% per degree-day, with $p = \SaaSixP$. Under that rule, the threat is live. It also bears on the replication decade, which rests on the older release.

A second mechanism works the other way. In the main sample, irrigated yields are almost always whole numbers, while the published production of rainfed maize is often not its yield times its area: in \SplitDerivOld\,\% of the pairs of 2000--2009 and \SplitDerivNew\,\% of those of 2010--2025, the rainfed yield is computed from production, in the older release partly as what remains of the total once irrigated maize is counted. If the irrigated yield entered by the statistical services responded too little to heat, a rainfed yield computed as a remainder would absorb the difference, and the gap would widen in hot summers for reasons unrelated to the harvest: this bias works in favour of the result. The published irrigated yield does respond to heat (about $-$\QoneIrrigatedPct\,\% per degree-day), so it is not frozen. Checks added later, computed after approximate results were known, do not show such an artefact. Without the pairs whose rainfed yield is computed, the estimate is \SplitNoDerivPct\,\% per degree-day ($p = \SplitNoDerivP$, \SplitNoDerivN{} pairs); on those pairs alone it is \SplitDerivPct\,\% ($p = \SplitDerivP$), and the two slopes do not differ detectably (two-sided $p = \SplitInterP$). On total yields, which do not use the split, departments with a larger irrigated share have yields less sensitive to heat (interaction \TotalInter, one-sided $p = \TotalInterP$), consistent with the result but identified from differences between departments. What these checks cannot detect is a split set by expert judgement on both modes at once: if the services expected irrigated maize to resist heat, the published gap would show it. Silage maize does not help here, since its split partly copies that of grain. These two mechanisms remain the main limitation of the outcome variable.

\subsection{How far the result travels}

The main sample covers \MainShareArea\,\% of the maize area of the population, and within it the significance of the association rests on the six departments with uplands: without them it is not significant, although their slope does not differ detectably from that of the others (\cref{subsec:maize-measure}). It is concentrated in Alsace, the Rhône--Alpes and Auvergne area, the Centre and Burgundy--Franche-Comté. It excludes the South-West (Landes, Pyrénées-Atlantiques, Gers, Lot-et-Garonne, among others), France's main maize basin, and the Centre-West (Charente, Charente-Maritime, Deux-Sèvres, Vendée, Vienne), whose irrigated series have a lower quality status. With all 38 departments, the estimate is larger (\QoneAllDepsPct\,\% per degree-day) and significant, which is reassuring but rests on data the statistics office itself rates lower; the slope of the 21 departments with a lower status (\StatusTwoPct\,\% per degree-day, one-sided $p = \StatusTwoP$) does not differ detectably from that of the main sample (two-sided $p = \StatusInterP$). Beyond grain maize, the same association appears for silage maize in \FodderDeps{} departments over 2010--2025 (\FodderPct\,\% per degree-day), so it is not specific to one crop, although the statistics of silage maize partly copy those of grain maize (\cref{subsec:maize-repl}).

The estimand also limits extrapolation in time. It describes the response to a hot summer given current varieties, practices and water rules. A lasting rise in temperature would bring adaptation, in sowing dates, varieties and the extent of irrigation, and possibly tighter water restrictions. Whether panel estimates of this kind approximate such long-run responses depends on conditions studied by \textcite{merel2021}; I do not claim that they hold here.

How hot will summers get? Bias-corrected projections for France suggest that the summers on which the result rests would become ordinary \parencite{drias2022}. For the \DriasModels{} regional simulations of the Explore2 ensemble, on the same 8~km grid and the same maize mask, I computed degree-days above 32\,$^{\circ}$C in July--August exactly as for the observations. \Cref{fig:projections} sets them against the observed summers. Over 1976--2005, the simulations give \DriasCtlMin{} to \DriasCtlMax{} degree-days against \DriasCtlSim{} for SIM2, so I measure changes against each simulation's own past. At the warming levels of the French adaptation trajectory, the average summer, taken over each simulation's window and then as the median across simulations, reaches \DriasDdTwo{} degree-days at +2.0\,$^{\circ}$C (range across simulations \DriasDdTwoMin--\DriasDdTwoMax), \DriasDdTwoSeven{} at +2.7\,$^{\circ}$C (\DriasDdTwoSevenMin--\DriasDdTwoSevenMax) and \DriasDdFour{} at +4.0\,$^{\circ}$C (\DriasDdFourMin--\DriasDdFourMax), against \DriasDdRef{} in 1976--2005 and \DriasObsMean{} on average over 2000--2025. At +2.7\,$^{\circ}$C this average summer is hotter than every summer of 2000--2025 except 2003; at +4.0\,$^{\circ}$C it is hotter than 2003 (\DriasObsThree). These are averages: individual summers vary around them, so that even at +4.0\,$^{\circ}$C many summers would remain cooler than 2003. Multiplying the median change by $\hat\beta$ gives a gap between irrigated and rainfed maize larger by about \DriasGapTwo\,\%, \DriasGapTwoSeven\,\% and \DriasGapFour\,\% (90\,\% interval of $\beta$: \DriasGapTwoSevenLow--\DriasGapTwoSevenHigh\,\% at +2.7\,$^{\circ}$C). The vapour pressure deficit rises as well, by a median \DriasVpdTwoSeven~kPa at +2.7\,$^{\circ}$C. These figures are not forecasts. They hold current practices fixed, ignore any change in irrigation or water rules, and at +4.0\,$^{\circ}$C apply a slope estimated on a single summer as hot as the projected average one. Three features of the estimate bear on these figures, in directions that do not agree. First, $\hat\beta$ is identified from differences between departments within a year, while the projected warming is common to all of them; in 2003, the linear model applied in this way predicted a wider gap than was observed (\LinPredThree{} against \ObsThree{} in log terms; with the weights of the valuation, the formula exceeds the value implied by the observed gap by \ValOverPct\,\%, or \ValOverNoRepPct\,\% without the pairs carried over from 2002, \cref{subsec:maize-value}). Second, $\hat\beta$ is measured at a given soil wetness, and drier soils are associated with a wider gap ($\hat\delta = \QoneSwi$). A later check takes this into account: the same simulations, run through the SIM2 land-surface model and distributed by DRIAS-Eau \parencite{driaseau2024}, give a July--August soil wetness index that changes by a median of $\SoilDeltaTwo$, $\SoilDeltaTwoSeven$ and $\SoilDeltaFour$ at the three warming levels (their reference level, \SoilRefSwi, is a little below the \SoilObsSwi{} observed in 1976--2005, so only changes are used). Adding $\hat\delta$ times this change raises the median gap to about \SoilGapTwo\,\%, \SoilGapTwoSeven\,\% and \SoilGapFour\,\% (\SoilGapFourMin--\SoilGapFourMax\,\% across simulations at +4.0\,$^{\circ}$C): drier soils widen the projected gap, but do not change its order of magnitude, although climate projections of soil moisture disagree with one another \parencite{rigden2020}. Third, the scaling uses the coefficient of degree-days, while in a model with the vapour pressure deficit the association moves to the drying power of the air (\cref{subsec:maize-vpd}). The first would make the figures too high, the second makes them, once included, a little higher; the net direction is not known, and the sensitivity to heat itself may change over time, as it has for maize in the United States Midwest \parencite{raghuram2026}. The percentages are therefore orders of magnitude, not projections of the gap.

\subsection{What would settle the open questions}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A map of irrigated parcels.} Assigning heat separately to irrigated and rainfed fields would remove the main measurement limitation. The parcel register used here does not record irrigation; maps derived from Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 exist for some basins, not for the whole country.
  \item \textbf{Surface temperature on irrigated and rainfed fields separately.} The MODIS check above averages both modes within each pixel; combined with a map of irrigated parcels, surface temperature would show directly whether irrigated fields stay cooler in hot summers \parencite{zhu2022}.
  \item \textbf{How the SAA splits irrigated and rainfed maize.} The public descriptions of the SAA do not say whether ``irrigated'' means irrigated that year or equipped for irrigation, nor whether the older release carried the split over from one year to the next, as the repetitions found above suggest (\cref{subsec:discussion-wrong}). The statistical service could answer it, and its farm-practice surveys, which record irrigation and yield field by field, could validate the split at the regional level.
  \item \textbf{Data on maize moved from grain to silage in dry years.} The SAA areas of silage maize show no shift over 2010--2025; field-level data on the harvest decision, and data covering 2003, would test the main selection threat directly.
  \item \textbf{Water volumes per crop.} The withdrawal volumes used here cover all irrigated crops, and the value per cubic metre in \cref{sec:maize} splits them by area. Volumes recorded per crop, or meters linked to parcels, would turn that descriptive figure into a measure of the productivity of water.
  \item \textbf{More hot summers.} The information on heat grows only when hot summers occur. Re-estimating the model as new harvests are published, with the specification unchanged, is the cleanest test of the result. The hot summer of 2026 is a first opportunity (\cref{subsec:maize-repl}); its definitive statistics are published about a year after the harvest.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What the way of working taught}

Three lessons are independent of the results.

\paragraph{Check the size of your tests on your data.} Standard errors clustered by department would have made the wheat result look solid ($p = \WheatPdep$ instead of $\WheatPwcr$); clustering by year, or by department and year, would not have ($p = \WheatPyear$ and \WheatPtwo), and on these panels their size stays within 10\,\%. What matters is to allow for the shock common to each year, and to check the size on the data. A placebo with permuted years costs a few minutes and changed the conclusions of this project. It does not detect every problem (serial correlation is one it cannot see), but it is a check that panels with common shocks should not skip. Nor should a single valid test be the end of it: when one year dominates, as 2003 does, other valid inferences can disagree, and reporting them is part of the result.

\paragraph{Choose the benchmark before the model.} In forecasting, the planned trend benchmark was weaker than the plain department mean. Judged against it, a model looked useful when it was not. The comparison that matters is with the simplest forecast a user would otherwise make.

\paragraph{Fixed rules help, within limits.} Writing the plan and its decision rule before estimating made it visible, and costly, to choose the variant that worked: the wheat result without 2025 was significant in the one-sided test and was not retained. It did not make the work blind, and no independent record dates the plans. The maize question was chosen after an exploratory analysis, some checks were added after results were known, and the two-sided interval for the wheat hypothesis, required by the plan, was computed late. After all results, every random seed was also changed; the first wheat forecast for 2026 is kept beside the recomputed one and remains the one the test fixed for future harvests will use, and later forecasts will use its seed. That forecast was computed in the run that read the early estimates of the 2026 harvest, so it is not blind. Some readings moved with the seed (\cref{app:deviations}), a reminder that results at the edge of a threshold are fragile to Monte Carlo choices. Saying so, and listing every change (\cref{app:deviations}), is part of what makes the remaining claims credible.
