\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

In 17 French departments over 26 summers, hot summers are associated with a wider yield advantage of irrigated over rainfed grain maize: \QonePct\,\% per degree-day above 32\,$^{\circ}$C in July and August, with a test whose size was checked on the same data. Rainfed yields are about \QoneRatio{} times more sensitive to heat than irrigated yields, which are themselves not immune. The association met the study's decision rule, but most of its sample comes from the years that suggested it. On the decade the exploratory analysis had not used, it kept its sign with weak evidence, at about half the 2010--2025 estimate because of 2003. It did not change with an older map of maize or with station-based temperatures, it replicated on silage maize (\FodderPct\,\% per degree-day, $p = \FodderP$), whose statistics partly copy those of grain maize, heat of the following summer showed no association, all \MultiN{} combinations of the main analytical choices gave a positive estimate, and it stays significant whichever single department or year is dropped, including 2003, whose removal raises it, although that summer carries \LevThree\,\% of the useful variation in heat. But it is not significant without the six departments with uplands, although their slope does not differ detectably from that of the others, nor with a cluster jackknife read against a Student distribution ($p = \CvThreeP$), although it is when that statistic is bootstrapped ($p = \CvBootP$). Its 90\,\% interval, \CiNinetyLow{} to \CiNinetyHigh\,\% per degree-day, supports the sign more than any precise magnitude: the sign is robust, its significance fragile. It depends on measuring heat where maize is grown, and in the earlier decade it does not hold with department-wide weather. With the vapour pressure deficit in the model, the association moves to the drying power of the air, which also tracks average heat, so it concerns hot, dry summers more than extreme heat as such, although silage maize does not confirm this split. Part of the irrigated--rainfed split of the older statistics seems to be carried over from year to year rather than observed, and a dedicated check classes this as a live threat; the rainfed yield is also often computed from production, which could bias the estimate upwards, and although later checks do not show this, a split judged by experts on both modes would not be detectable. It is an association, not the causal effect of irrigation. In bias-corrected projections, the hot summers on which it rests become common at the warming levels of the French adaptation trajectory: the average summer of the median simulation brings \DriasDdTwoSeven{} degree-days at +2.7\,$^{\circ}$C and \DriasDdFour{} at +4.0\,$^{\circ}$C, against \DriasObsThree{} in 2003, with individual summers spread around these averages, so the question will matter more; but the estimate says nothing direct about how irrigated maize will fare then, after adaptation and under the water rules of that time. Early estimates for the hot summer of 2026, the second hottest of the series, point the same way, but they are provisional; the test fixed for future harvests and registered on OSF (\href{https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/QKNAV}{doi:10.17605/OSF.IO/QKNAV}), which needs the definitive statistics of five harvests (2026--2030), will give the first confirmatory evidence. The planned interaction with crisis-level water restrictions has the opposite sign to the hypothesis.

The other questions gave null results. For wheat over 1959--2025, heat at the end of the cycle is associated with lower yields, but this cannot be distinguished from zero, and the data, in which each period holds only one or a few very hot summers, cannot tell whether its sensitivity has changed over time. Forecasts of wheat yields from weather and satellite data, built according to the protocol, are not significantly better than the historical mean of each department.

The methodological lesson is not new, but it changed the conclusions here. In panels where a weather shock hits many units in the same year, standard errors clustered by unit, or Conley standard errors as implemented here, can reject a true null in a large share of cases: \UsualSizeMin\,\% to \UsualSizeMax\,\% on these three panels. Inferences that allow for the shock common to each year did much better: clustering by year or by department and year stayed within 10\,\%, and the bootstrap by year closest to 5\,\%. A placebo that keeps the real geography of the shock and permutes years reveals the problem in minutes. The code for this check is released with the monograph, so that it can be run on other data before their conclusions are drawn.
