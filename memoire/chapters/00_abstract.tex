\begin{abstract}
\noindent Does the yield advantage of irrigated maize grow in hot summers? French agricultural statistics report grain maize yields \emph{separately} for irrigated and rainfed land, by department. I relate the gap between the two, 2000--2025, to degree-days above 32\,$^{\circ}$C in July--August, computed on an 8~km grid and averaged with weights given by the area of maize parcels. The hypothesis was suggested by an exploratory analysis of 2010--2025; the decade 2000--2009, which that analysis did not use, serves as a replication. In 17 departments outside the South-West (\QoneN{} department-years), each degree-day is associated with a \QonePct\,\% higher ratio of irrigated to rainfed yield, so that the median advantage of \GapMedianPct\,\% would become \GapMedianAfterPct\,\% (one-sided wild cluster bootstrap by year, $p = \QoneP$; measured size \QoneSizeWCR\,\%; 90\,\% interval \CiNinetyLow{} to \CiNinetyHigh\,\%). This meets the study's decision rule: the sign is robust, but its significance is fragile, for four reasons. \ExploSharePct\,\% of the sample comes from the exploratory years, and the new decade keeps the sign at about half the 2010--2025 estimate ($p = \QoneReplP$). Without the six departments with uplands, or with heat averaged over whole departments instead of maize parcels ($p = \QoneAreaP$), the result is not significant; a cluster-jackknife statistic is not significant against a Student distribution ($p = \CvThreeP$) but is when bootstrapped ($p = \CvBootP$, size \CvBootSize\,\%). In the older release of the statistics, the irrigated-to-rainfed ratio repeats from one year to the next more often than chance, as an imputation would make it, and a dedicated check classes this as a live threat; the rainfed yield is also often computed from production, which could bias the result upwards, although checks added later do not show it. And with the vapour pressure deficit in the model, the association moves to the drying power of the air; since that deficit is computed from the daily maximum temperature, the data cannot tell extreme heat, average heat and dry air apart, and the result concerns hot, dry summers rather than extreme heat as such. It holds with station-based temperatures, points the same way with satellite surface temperature, which also reflects the water stress of the crop, replicates on silage maize, whose statistics partly copy those of grain, and all \MultiN{} specifications of a multiverse analysis give a positive estimate; early, coarse estimates for the hot summer of 2026 point the same way, and a test fixed for the 2026--2030 harvests will give the first confirmatory evidence. An interaction with water restrictions has the opposite sign to the hypothesis. The result is an association, not the causal effect of irrigation. A methodological check changed the conclusions: standard errors clustered by department, or Conley, reject a true null in \UsualSizeMin\,\% to \UsualSizeMax\,\% of placebo simulations on these panels, and with them heat would have appeared ``significantly'' associated with lower wheat yields ($p = \WheatPdep$, against $p = \WheatPwcr$ with a correctly sized test); clustering by year, or by department and year, would also have avoided this error. Wheat yields over 1959--2025 and out-of-sample forecasts give null results, reported in full.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} heat stress; irrigation; maize; France; panel data; wild cluster bootstrap; test size.\\
\textbf{JEL codes:} Q15, Q54, C23, C12.
\end{abstract}
