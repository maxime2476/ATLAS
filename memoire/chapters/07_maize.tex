\section{Results: heat and the irrigated--rainfed maize gap}
\label{sec:maize}

This section reports the main result, then asks four questions about it: how strong the statistical evidence is, whether it holds on years never used, whether it depends on how heat is measured, and whether it is driven by particular departments or by a change in what is harvested. All tests are those of the plan unless they are marked \emph{added later}. \Cref{app:deviations} lists the added checks; none of them changes the status of a main hypothesis.

\subsection{The data at a glance}

In the main sample (\MaizeMainDeps{} departments, \MaizeMainPairs{} department-years), irrigated grain maize yields \YieldIrr{} quintals per hectare on average, against \YieldRain{} for rainfed maize. The median gap is \GapMedianPct\,\%, and the irrigated yield is higher than the rainfed one in \GapAboveCount{} of the \MaizeMainPairs{} department-years, equal in \GapEqualCount{} and lower in \GapBelowCount. The question is not whether irrigated maize yields more, but how this advantage moves with summer heat.

\paragraph{H1 is established under the decision rule.} Each degree-day above 32\,$^{\circ}$C in July--August is associated with a \QonePct\,\% wider yield gap in favour of irrigated maize ($p = \QoneP$). The measured size of this test on the same data is \QoneSizeWCR\,\%, below the 10\,\% limit. ``Established'' is meant in the sense of this rule only. \ExploPairs{} of the \MaizeMainPairs{} department-years (\ExploSharePct\,\%) belong to 2010--2025, the period whose exploratory analysis suggested the hypothesis; the only new evidence is the decade 2000--2009 (\cref{subsec:maize-repl}).

\paragraph{The association in the data.} \Cref{fig:maize-resid} shows what the estimate rests on. Each point is a department-year, with the gap and the heat both taken net of the department and year effects and of soil wetness; the slope of the cloud is exactly $\hat\beta$. Most summers bring little heat beyond what the year effects absorb. Many of the extreme heat residuals come from 2003, which carries \LevThree\,\% of the residual variation of heat (\cref{subsec:maize-inference}); not all of its points lie above the line, which is why the estimate is larger without that summer (\cref{tab:maize-rob}).

\paragraph{How precise is the estimate?} Inverting the bootstrap (added later) gives a 90\,\% interval for $\beta$ of \CiNinetyLow{} to \CiNinetyHigh\,\% per degree-day (95\,\%: \CiNinetyFiveLow{} to \CiNinetyFiveHigh\,\%). The sign is supported; the magnitude is known only to within a factor of eight. With the same placebo as the size check, the test detects a true effect of the estimated size (0.016) in \PowerFull\,\% of cases and half of it in \PowerHalf\,\% (added later; size under the null in this run: \PowerCheckSize\,\%).

\paragraph{Both modes lose, rainfed maize much more.} Each degree-day is associated with about \QoneRainfedPct\,\% lower rainfed yields and about \QoneIrrigatedPct\,\% lower irrigated yields; both are significant after the Holm correction, and the size of these two tests, measured later with the same placebo as H1, is \HtwobSize\,\% and \HtwocSize\,\%. On these point estimates, rainfed maize is about \QoneRatio{} times more sensitive to heat; this ratio of two imprecise coefficients has no interval of its own and should be read as ``several times''. Irrigation is therefore associated with a smaller sensitivity, not with its disappearance. Wetter soils are associated with a narrower gap ($\hat\delta = \QoneSwi$ per unit of the soil wetness index), as expected if the advantage of irrigated maize comes from water.

\paragraph{Water restrictions (H3).} The slope of the gap on heat is not smaller where crisis-level water restrictions lasted longer; the interaction even has the opposite sign ($p_{\text{Holm}} = \QoneHthreeP$). This result is not interpretable causally. Restrictions are decided during the driest summers and in the driest departments, so their duration is tied to the very conditions that drive the gap. Which mechanism produces the positive interaction is a question I did not test. Possible ones, none checked: restrictions are decided where the gap is already widening; the indicator counts days with at least one zone of the department at crisis level, which does not say whether irrigated maize was restricted; and orders may exempt some uses. The indicator is also coarse, and the sample has only 10 summers. For water policy, this is the most directly relevant result of the monograph, and it does not show that restrictions reduced the advantage of irrigated maize.

\subsection{How strong is the statistical evidence?}
\label{subsec:maize-inference}

\Cref{sec:inference} showed why the bootstrap by year is the reference test on these panels. Its validity, however, rests on the clusters, and one cluster dominates: in the weighted model, 2003 alone carries \LevThree\,\% of the residual variation of heat ($N_{\mathrm{eff}} = \NeffLevAll$; the share of \NeffMaizeShareTwoThousandThree\,\% in \cref{sec:exposure} is computed without weights or the soil wetness index). With so much leverage in one year, it is worth asking whether other valid procedures agree. \Cref{tab:maize-inference} reports three, all added later.

The procedures do not agree on the strength of the evidence. Randomisation over permuted years gives a smaller $p$-value, but its null, like the placebo's, leaves the true effect of heat in the error. The cluster jackknife, which \textcite{mackinnon2023} recommend when a few clusters carry much of the leverage, gives $p = \CvThreeP$: with it, H1 would not be significant at 5\,\%. Its size, measured afterwards with the same placebo, is \CvThreeSize\,\% (Monte Carlo standard error \CvThreeSizeMc{} points), against \QoneSizeWCR\,\% for the bootstrap on the same permutations, and its power against a true effect of the estimated size is \CvThreePowFull\,\%, the same as the bootstrap's. The jackknife is therefore not a more conservative test on these data, and its disagreement with the bootstrap is not explained by a lack of power. The disagreement comes from the reference distribution instead. 2003 has a partial leverage of \LevThree\,\%, \LevTwoThousandThreeRatio{} times the average share of a year \parencite{mackinnon2023summclust}, and \LevSecondYear{} comes next with \LevSecond\,\%. With one cluster of such leverage, the jackknife statistic ($t = \CvThreeT$) need not follow a Student distribution. Bootstrapping that same statistic \parencite{mackinnon2023jae} gives a one-sided $p = \CvBootP$, with a size of \CvBootSize\,\% measured on the same placebo (added later). The decision rule of phase 4 bis rests on the bootstrap and is unchanged, and the result is significant under the two bootstrap tests but not under the jackknife read against a Student distribution. Two further diagnostics support the bootstrap itself: a placebo that keeps each summer's heat in its own year and shuffles it across departments gives a size of \WithinYearSize\,\%, and the usual placebo shows no abnormal rejection rate according to the year that receives the field of 2003. In the same placebo runs, \texttt{fixest} had to correct the Conley matrix in \ConleyFixedPct\,\% of repetitions, which is one more reason not to rely on it. Dropping one year at a time, finally, leaves the estimate between \LoyoYearMinPct{} and \LoyoYearMaxPct\,\% per degree-day and significant in \LoyoYearSig{} cases out of \LoyoYearN; the largest change comes from dropping 2003, which raises it.

\subsection{Replication on years never used}
\label{subsec:maize-repl}

The exploratory analysis that motivated this question had used 2010--2025. On 2000--2009 alone, a decade that no earlier analysis had touched, the estimate keeps its sign: \QoneReplPct\,\% per degree-day, with a one-sided $p = \QoneReplP$. The same mask gives \QoneRecentPct\,\% on 2010--2025, so the estimate for the earlier decade is about half as large. On 2000--2009 without 2003, the point estimate is larger than on 2010--2025 (\cref{tab:maize-measure}): the smaller estimate over the decade comes from 2003, which raises a question about linearity, taken up below.

Four cautions apply. The two-sided $p$-value is \QoneReplPtwo. The test rests on 10 years: measured later with the same placebo, the size of the bootstrap with 10 clusters is \ReplSize\,\%, but its power against a true effect of the estimated size is only \ReplPower\,\%, so a $p$-value near 0.05 is weak evidence. Within the decade, 2003 carries \LevThreeEarly\,\% of the residual variation of heat ($N_{\mathrm{eff}} = \NeffLevEarly$), so the replication is essentially a test on one summer. And, as shown below, the replication holds with the maize mask but not with department-wide weather. I read it as consistent in sign, not as a confirmation of magnitude. An interaction between heat and the decade 2000--2009 (added later) finds no detectable difference between the two slopes ($\DecDiff$, two-sided $p = \DecDiffP$; without 2003, $\DecDiffNoThree$, $p = \DecDiffNoThreeP$): the data cannot say whether the earlier decade is really less sensitive.

\paragraph{Another crop: silage maize.} A different test of the same idea, added later, uses a crop the hypothesis was never estimated on. The SAA also reports, for 2010--2025, the yields of silage maize on irrigated and on rainfed land. Applying the population rule of grain maize gives \FodderDeps{} departments (only five of them with quality status~1, so all are kept and the data are weaker), \FodderN{} department-years and a median gap of \FodderGap\,\%. With heat measured on the silage-maize parcels of the RPG and the same model, each degree-day above 32\,$^{\circ}$C is associated with a gap wider by \FodderPct\,\% (one-sided $p = \FodderP$; measured size \FodderSize\,\%), almost exactly the grain-maize estimate. By the criterion of this check, the association replicates on silage maize. It is not an independent sample of summers, since the years overlap with those of grain maize, nor a fully independent measurement. The crop, its harvest and its mask are different, but its statistics come from the same survey and the same services, and in \FodderSameN{} of the \FodderPairsN{} department-years shared with grain maize the irrigated-to-rainfed ratio of silage, rounded to the hundredth, is exactly that of grain, against about \FodderSameRef{} expected by chance (95th percentile \FodderSameRefP), as if silage yields had been derived from grain yields with a coefficient. Without these department-years (added later), the estimate is \FodderNoSamePct\,\% ($p = \FodderNoSameP$). The replication is on another crop, not on another measurement of the split. With the five departments of status~1 only, the estimate is \FodderSOnePct\,\% ($p = \FodderSOneP$; five departments only, bootstrap by year).

\paragraph{A first look at 2026.} The summer of 2026 brought \TwentySixHeat{} degree-days above 32\,$^{\circ}$C in the main sample, \TwentySixHeatDev{} more than the departments' 2000--2025 average and second only to 2003. Only the definitive statistics for that harvest, published about a year later, can enter the test fixed for future harvests. The early estimates of the statistics office, made while maize was still being harvested \parencite{agreste2026conjoncture}, allow an exploratory look. They are coarse: rainfed yields are set at \TwentySixRainMin~q/ha in \TwentySixRainMinN{} departments, and the ratio of irrigated to rainfed yield ranges from \TwentySixRatioMin{} to \TwentySixRatioMax{}, for a median gap of \TwentySixGapMed\,\%. On these figures, the gap widened far more than the linear estimate predicts: it exceeded each department's average by \TwentySixGapObs{} in log terms, against \TwentySixGapPred{} predicted by $\hat\beta$, the reverse of 2003. Across the 17 departments of 2026 alone, the slope of the gap on heat is \TwentySixSlopePct\,\% per degree-day, near the upper end of the interval of $\beta$; with 2026 added, model A1 gives \TwentySixAllPct\,\% ($p = \TwentySixAllP$). By the criterion of this look, the new summer is consistent with H1. It is one summer, its rainfed yields look like expert judgements made before the harvest was over, and the figures will be revised.

\paragraph{Soil moisture and other weather controls.} Three checks, added later, ask whether the result depends on how the dryness of the summer is controlled for. Without the soil wetness index, the estimate is \NoSwiPct\,\% ($p = \NoSwiP$); with the July--August climatic water balance (precipitation minus evapotranspiration) in its place, \PetPct\,\% ($p = \PetP$). By its criterion, the association does not depend on the moisture control chosen. A partially linear model estimated by double machine learning \parencite{chernozhukov2018}, in which soil moisture, precipitation, evapotranspiration and growing degree-days enter through random forests with folds made of whole years, gives a median $\hat\beta = \DmlBeta$ over 50 random splits of the years into folds (one-sided $p = \DmlPtwo$, twice the median $p$-value of the splits, the valid way to combine them \parencite{meinshausen2009}; the median itself is \DmlP, and $p < 0.05$ in \DmlShareSig\,\% of the splits). A single split is not enough: across splits, $\hat\beta$ ranges from \DmlBetaMin{} to \DmlBetaMax. The median is \DmlGapPct\,\% below the main estimate, at the limit of the 15\,\% set for calling it almost unchanged, and just past it ($\DmlGapExact\,\%$); with other random seeds, it was 12\,\% below, with a combined $p$ of 0.044. Both readings are therefore sensitive to the seed: flexible controls lower the estimate by about an eighth to a sixth and leave it at the edge of significance. This version also removes department and year means from all variables before the folds are formed, so the transformed values of one fold depend on the other folds, and forests applied to demeaned controls do not estimate a partially linear model with additive fixed effects. A variant that removes only the year means (which stay within folds made of whole years) and gives the forests the department means of the controls and of heat, in the manner of \textcite{mundlak1978}, gives a median of \DmlMundBeta{} (\DmlMundGapPct\,\% below the main estimate, combined one-sided $p = \DmlMundPtwo$). The double machine learning estimates thus point the same way as the main model, smaller, and do not confirm it on their own. None of these models separates heat from drought, however: within departments, degree-days above 32\,$^{\circ}$C and the water balance are correlated at $\CorrPet$ (\cref{sec:discussion}).

\paragraph{Heat at the wrong time.} If the association reflected a persistent difference between departments rather than the summer of the harvest, heat measured at other times would show it too (added later). The heat of the following summer, which cannot affect the harvest, shows no detectable association with the gap (two-sided $p = \LeadP$; its point estimate has the opposite sign), and the main coefficient is unchanged when it is added (\LeadMainPct\,\%). The heat of the previous summer shows none either ($p = \LagP$); the power of these two checks was not measured. Within the season, the July--August coefficient stays significant when June and September heat enter the same model (\JaCalPct\,\%, $p = \JaCalP$). June heat has a large but imprecise coefficient (\JunePct\,\% per degree-day, $p = \JuneP$): June rarely exceeds 32\,$^{\circ}$C (\JuneMean{} degree-days on average, against \JaMean{} in July--August), and its few hot Junes are also hot summers. September is no test at all: it almost never exceeds 32\,$^{\circ}$C in these departments (\SeptMean{} degree-days on average).

\label{subsec:maize-measure}

The divergence is concentrated in 2000--2009 ($\MaskEarly$ with the maize mask, $\AreaEarly$ with department area) and persists in that decade even without 2003. On 2010--2025, department-wide weather gives a \emph{larger} coefficient (\AreaRecent{} against \MaskRecent).

What we know about the two measures is the following.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The age of the mask is not the cause.} A mask drawn from 2008 blocks gives almost the same exposure as the 2015--2017 mask and the same estimate.
  \item \textbf{The temperature source is not the cause.} Recomputed from E-OBS, a grid interpolated from station records \parencite{cornes2018}, with the same double-sine method, window and maize mask (added later), heat is correlated at \EobsCorr{} with the SIM2 measure and gives \EobsPct\,\% per degree-day ($p = \EobsP$). E-OBS counts more degree-days above 32\,$^{\circ}$C, so the coefficients per degree-day are not directly comparable: one within-department standard deviation of heat goes with a gap wider by \EobsSdEffect\,\% with E-OBS and \SimSdEffect\,\% with SIM2. The check is partial: some French stations behind E-OBS also feed SAFRAN, only the main model was re-estimated, and the soil wetness control still comes from SIM2. With the average of the two measures as heat (added later), the estimate is \AvgHeatPct\,\% per degree-day (one-sided $p = \AvgHeatP$; measured size \AvgHeatSize\,\%).
  \item \textbf{Satellite surface temperature points the same way.} The mean daytime land-surface temperature of July--August, averaged over each 8~km cell and then weighted by maize area as for degree-days, from MODIS 8-day composites starting in July--August (so covering about 3 July to 4 September) \parencite{wan2021} (added later; protocol fixed before the data were received), is correlated at \LstCorr{} with the degree-days within departments. Used in place of them, each additional degree Celsius of surface temperature goes with a gap wider by \LstPct\,\% ($p = \LstP$). The satellite measure is taken around 10:30, not at the daily maximum, and the cell average mixes maize, which covers a small share of the land on average, with other crops and land cover, and irrigated with rainfed fields. Surface temperature also rises when the crop is short of water, so it measures the dryness of the summer as much as its heat, and it does not confirm the degree-day result independently.
  \item \textbf{The department-wide measure understates the 2003 heat where maize grows in departments with uplands.} In \ReliefCount{} such departments, maize fields lie \ReliefAltDiffMin{} to \ReliefAltDiffMax{}~m below the department's average altitude, and department-wide weather understates their 2003 heat by up to \ReliefGapMax{} degree-days (\cref{tab:relief}). This is shown for 2003 only, while the divergence persists on 2000--2009 without 2003. An error that scaled heat by the same factor every year would rescale $\beta$, not remove it; removing the association requires errors that reorder departments from one year to the next, which is not shown.
  \item \textbf{Neither measure is checked against where each mode grows.} Both masks assign the same heat to irrigated and rainfed maize. In departments with uplands, irrigated maize may sit lower in the valleys than rainfed maize, so the mask may describe the heat of irrigated fields better than that of rainfed ones.
  \item \textbf{The maize mask is the main measure of the plan}, which, according to the author, was written before any estimate on 2000--2009.
  \item \textbf{The choice did not favour the result already known.} When the specification was written, the only known estimate (phase 6, 2010--2025) used department-wide weather and was significant. On those same years the maize mask gives a smaller estimate (\MaskRecent{} against \AreaRecent).
\end{itemize}
These points favour the maize mask. They do not explain the whole divergence, nor why department-wide weather gives a stronger association over 2010--2025, and the altitude pattern was sought after the results were known.

\paragraph{How much does measurement error attenuate the estimate?} Errors in measured heat that are unrelated to the gap bias $\beta$ towards zero. With two independent measures of the same heat, one can instrument the other and remove that bias (added later). Instrumented by E-OBS, which is strongly related to the SIM2 measure (first-stage $F = \IvEobsF$, clusters by year), the coefficient becomes \IvEobsBeta, \IvEobsRatio{} times the least-squares estimate; the reduced form, the gap on E-OBS heat, is significant under the bootstrap ($p = \IvEobsRfP$). This correction assumes classical errors, unrelated to true heat; errors in a variable defined by a threshold are not of that kind, and the two grids share some stations, so its direction is not certain either. Instrumented by satellite surface temperature ($F = \IvLstF$), the ratio reaches \IvLstRatio, but surface temperature reflects the water stress of rainfed maize and possibly the cooling of irrigated fields, not only air heat, so that exclusion is doubtful. Other checks point the other way (the climatic water balance, double machine learning, the linear prediction of 2003), so the net direction of the bias is not known, within the wide interval of \cref{subsec:maize-main}.

\paragraph{The departments with uplands.} Because the measure matters most in these six departments, three checks, added later, ask how much the result rests on them. Dropping one department at a time leaves the estimate between \LooMinPct{} and \LooMaxPct\,\% per degree-day, significant in \LooSig{} cases out of 17. Dropping the six departments with uplands together gives \NoReliefPct\,\% with $p = \NoReliefP$ on the remaining 11: by the criterion of this check, H1 depends on these departments. But their slope does not differ detectably from that of the other departments (interaction $+\RelInter$, two-sided $p = \RelInterP$), so the loss of significance is consistent with the remaining 11 departments simply carrying less information. Either way, the significance of the result comes from the very departments where the choice of heat measure matters, so the measure and the sample cannot be separated. The honest summary is that the result holds when heat is measured where maize is grown, and that it is sensitive to that choice in the earlier decade and rests on the departments with uplands.


\subsection{Heat, dry air or dry soil?}
\label{subsec:maize-vpd}

Hot French summers are also dry ones, and the literature suggests that much of the damage attributed to heat runs through water: the evaporative demand of hot, dry air \parencite{lobell2013} and the combination of heat with dry soils \parencite{haqiqi2021,lesk2022}. Three checks, added later, look at these channels.

\paragraph{Vapour pressure deficit.} From the daily SIM2 temperatures and relative humidity, the vapour pressure deficit of the afternoon (saturation pressure at the daily maximum minus actual vapour pressure) is averaged over July--August on the maize mask. Used in place of degree-days, it is strongly associated with the gap: \VpdPct\,\% per kPa (one-sided $p = \VpdP$), or \VpdSdEffect\,\% for one within-department standard deviation (\VpdSd~kPa), against \SimSdEffect\,\% for degree-days. When both enter the same model, the vapour pressure deficit keeps its coefficient (\VpdJointPct\,\% per kPa, two-sided $p = \VpdJointP$) and the degree-days above 32\,$^{\circ}$C lose theirs ($\VpdJointHeatPct$\,\%, $p = \VpdJointHeatP$). The two are correlated at \VpdCorr{} within departments, so by the criterion of this check they cannot be separated with these data. But the pattern is the one \textcite{lobell2013} describe: what this monograph measures as extreme heat behaves like the drying power of hot air. Because the deficit is computed from the daily maximum temperature, it also tracks the average heat of the summer, so the data cannot tell either whether the association concerns extreme heat, average heat or dry air. The pattern is not confirmed on silage maize: there the vapour pressure deficit alone is associated with the gap (one-sided $p = \FodderVpdP$), but in the joint model neither it nor the degree-days remains significant ($p = \FodderVpdJointP$ and $\FodderHeatJointP$), so the reading rests on grain maize alone.

\paragraph{Heat on dry soils.} Adding an interaction between degree-days and soil wetness gives the expected sign, a larger effect of heat when soils are drier, but it is not detectable (interaction $\CompoundInter$, two-sided $p = \CompoundP$). In the same model, the slope of heat at average soil wetness is close to zero (\CompoundHeatPct\,\% per degree-day, $p = \CompoundHeatP$): the association appears in dry summers, a further reason to read it as concerning hot, dry summers. Hot summers are already dry ones in this sample, which leaves little variation to tell the two apart.

\paragraph{Do farmers pump more in hot summers?} The presumed mechanism behind the gap is that irrigated fields receive water that rainfed fields do not. Volumes declared for irrigation in the national water withdrawal database (BNPE), summed by department for 2012--2023, allow a check of the first link. With the same fixed effects and weights, they rise with soil dryness (coefficient $\VolSwi$ on the soil wetness index, two-sided $p = \VolSwiP$, \VolN{} department-years) but not detectably with degree-days above 32\,$^{\circ}$C (\VolHeatPct\,\% per degree-day, one-sided $p = \VolHeatP$; one-sided $p = \VolVpdP$ for the vapour pressure deficit). The volumes cover all irrigated crops, and in hot summers some withdrawals are restricted, which may cap the response. The check therefore says that water use follows dry soils; it does not say that the gap is caused by the extra water.

\subsection{Is the slope the same everywhere?}
\label{subsec:maize-heterogeneity}

If irrigation protects against heat, the gap might widen more where more maize is irrigated or where the irrigated land differs most from the rest. Two approaches, added later and detailed in \cref{app:maize-checks}, look for such differences; neither finds one. The interaction of heat with the irrigated share of grain maize is not detectable ($p = \IrrInterP$), and department-specific slopes do not differ detectably once the test accounts for their strong correlation, calibrated by the bootstrap by year ($p = \HomogP$). With 17 departments, the data cannot say where the association is stronger.

\subsection{Is the composition of land moving with heat?}
\label{subsec:maize-composition}

If hot years changed which land is irrigated, or which maize is harvested as grain, the gap could move for reasons unrelated to irrigation itself. Two descriptive checks on the areas reported by the SAA, not planned (\cref{app:maize-checks}), find no such response: neither the irrigated share of grain maize area ($p = \CompP$, standard errors clustered by year) nor the share of rainfed maize harvested as silage (one-sided $p = \SilageP$, 2010--2025 only) moves detectably with heat. Both checks have little power, and the absence of a detected response is not evidence that composition is stable.

\subsection{What it is worth}
\label{subsec:maize-value}

\Cref{tab:valuation} turns the main estimate into quantities for three hot summers. For each department, the extra production associated with irrigation \emph{in a summer hotter than the department's normal} is
\[
\text{irrigated area} \times \text{rainfed yield} \times \big[e^{\hat\beta\,(\ddthirtytwo - \text{department mean})} - 1\big],
\]
valued at the maize price of the year in current euros. Applying the factor to the rainfed yield is the cautious choice; applied to the irrigated yield, the 2003 figure would be about \ValAltPct\,\% higher. The formula extrapolates the linear estimate to 2003, far above the other summers. A quadratic term in degree-days, added later, has the sign of a slope that flattens at high heat but is not detectable (two-sided $p = \QuadSqP$), and it barely changes the predicted gap of 2003 (\QuadPredThree{} against \LinPredThree{} for the linear model and \ObsThree{} observed); degree-days split into 30--32, 32--34 and above 34\,$^{\circ}$C are too imprecise to show where the response steepens (largest, for 32--34\,$^{\circ}$C: \BinMidPct\,\% per degree-day, $p = \BinMidP$). It also applies to each summer's whole excess heat, national part included, a slope that is identified from differences between departments within a year; in the regression, the national part of each summer is absorbed by the year effects. A simple check suggests it overstates the 2003 figure: replacing $\hat\beta$ times each department's excess heat by the observed excess of its gap over its average, with the same weights, gives \QoneValObs{} instead of \QoneValTwoThousandThree{} million euros, so the formula is \ValOverPct\,\% higher. Part of the difference comes from the Loiret and the Eure-et-Loir, among the largest contributors, whose 2003 yields repeat those of 2002 in both modes, as carried-over values would; without these \ValRepPairs{} pairs, the formula is \ValOverNoRepPct\,\% higher than the observed gap. The comparison does not run one way: in 2019 and 2022 the observed gap gives more than the formula (\QoneValObsNineteen{} and \QoneValObsTwentyTwo{} million euros), and it also reflects everything else that moved the gap in a given year.

In the 17 departments, the extra production associated with irrigation in the summer of 2003 is worth between \QoneValRangeLow{} and \QoneValRangeHigh{} million euros. The upper end is the formula of \cref{tab:valuation} at the spliced price (\QoneValTwoThousandThree{} million euros; \QoneValLow{} to \QoneValHigh{} over the 90\,\% interval of $\hat\beta$). The lower end replaces the formula by the observed gap, and the spliced price of \PriceRaccordThree{} euros per quintal by the price Eurostat reports for 2003, \PriceEurostatThree{} euros (at that price the formula gives \QoneValEurostat{} million euros; the observed gap without the carried-over pairs gives \QoneValObsNoRep{} at the spliced price). The spliced price applies to 2003 a ratio between the INSEE price index and the Eurostat price fitted on 2012--2016, which is less stable before 2012, and which of the two prices is closer to what farmers received is not known. The volume given by the formula equals about \ValShareTwoThousandThree\,\% of all the grain maize, irrigated and rainfed, harvested in these departments that year (\ProdValTwoThousandThree{} million euros at the spliced price, \ProdValTwoThousandThreeEurostat{} at the Eurostat price). In 2022, fewer quintals are worth more than in 2019 because the price of maize was about twice as high. These figures are gross production values in current euros. They are not the total value of irrigation, which also includes its average advantage; not a value net of the cost of water, energy and labour; not a value of the water used (a rough one is given below) or the cost of a water restriction; and not a causal gain. They also leave out the part of the advantage associated with drier soils, since $\beta$ is measured at a given soil wetness.

\paragraph{A value per cubic metre.} A rough value per cubic metre is possible where grain maize dominates irrigation. In six departments of the main sample (01, 03, 18, 63, 67, 68), grain maize occupied more than half of the irrigated area in the 2020 agricultural census. There I attribute to maize the BNPE volume times that share and divide by the SAA irrigated maize area. The resulting dose has a median of \WatDose~m$^3$/ha over 2012--2023, close to the 1\,410~m$^3$/ha reported for grain maize in Occitanie in 2021 \parencite{agreste2026occitanie}. A plausibility rule (500 to 3\,000~m$^3$/ha) excludes \WatExcl{} of the \WatN{} department-years. Dividing the observed yield gap (median \WatGain~q/ha) by the dose gives about \WatKg~kg of grain and \WatEur{} euros of gross production per cubic metre (\WatEurTen{} with the 2010 shares; from \WatEurMin{} to \WatEurMax{} across departments). The lowest value comes from the Puy-de-Dôme, where the median dose over all years, \WatLowDepDose~m$^3$/ha, lies close to the upper bound of the plausibility rule, which excludes \WatLowDepExcl{} of its \WatLowDepYears{} years; without it, the range starts at \WatEurMinOther. For comparison, a study of irrigating maize farms in the Adour-Garonne basin estimates the margin attributable to irrigation water at 0.20 to 0.28 euros per cubic metre in 2012--2014, before depreciation of equipment, for 1.4~kg of maize per cubic metre \parencite{draaf2017}. A gross value should exceed a margin, and it does. The figure is descriptive: the observed gap also reflects differences between irrigated and rainfed land, and the dose assumes that maize receives as much water per hectare as the other irrigated crops of the department. If maize receives more, its dose is understated and the value per cubic metre overstated; that the median dose is close to the figure reported for Occitanie suggests that the error is not large on average. In hot summers the dose rises (\WatDoseSlope~m$^3$/ha per degree-day, two-sided bootstrap by year, $p = \WatDoseSlopeP$), but the gap per hectare does not rise detectably in these six departments (two-sided, $p = \WatGainSlopeP$), so the value of the extra water of hot summers cannot be measured this way. That the dose rises with heat while total volumes do not (\cref{subsec:maize-vpd}) is not a contradiction: the dose is measured in six departments where maize dominates irrigation, per hectare of irrigated maize, whereas the volumes are totals for all irrigated crops in 17 departments, in which crops less sensitive to heat may be watered less in hot summers or restricted first. With six departments, the dose slope is also fragile, and it is estimated on the years kept by the plausibility rule, which depends on the dose itself; I do not read more into it.

\subsection{Summary}

In the main sample, summer heat is associated with a wider yield advantage of irrigated over rainfed maize. The association satisfies the decision rule, although \ExploSharePct\,\% of its sample comes from the years that suggested it; keeps its sign, with weak evidence, on a decade the exploratory analysis had not used; survives an older parcel mask and station-based temperatures; points the same way, smaller and not confirmed on its own, with flexible weather controls (double machine learning); and replicates on silage maize, whose statistics partly copy those of grain maize. Heat of the following summer, which cannot affect the harvest, shows no detectable association with the gap, and not of the sign a persistent difference between departments would give. Its magnitude is uncertain and rests largely on 2003. It is not significant with a cluster jackknife read against a Student distribution ($p = \CvThreeP$), although it is when that statistic is bootstrapped ($p = \CvBootP$), nor without the six departments with uplands, although their slope does not differ detectably from that of the others. It depends on measuring heat where maize is grown, the main measure of the plan, supported by the geography of the departments concerned, but one that matters. Taken together, the checks added after the main estimates fall into three groups: most support the association (station-based temperatures, silage maize, heat at the wrong time, other moisture controls, the specification curve), some cannot inform it (satellite surface temperature, which also reflects water stress; slope heterogeneity; composition of land), and five qualify it (the cluster jackknife, the departments with uplands, double machine learning, the vapour pressure deficit, and the repeated ratios of the older statistics). The sign is robust; its significance is fragile, and the first confirmatory evidence will come from a test fixed for the 2026--2030 harvests. Its outcome is not fully measured either: part of the irrigated--rainfed split of the older release seems to be carried over from year to year (\cref{subsec:discussion-wrong}). The planned interaction with crisis-level water restrictions has the opposite sign to the hypothesis. It is an association, not the causal effect of irrigation.
