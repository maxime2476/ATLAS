Does the yield advantage of irrigated maize grow in hot summers? Using French department yields reported separately for irrigated and rainfed grain maize (2000-2025) and heat on an 8 km grid weighted by maize parcels, I compare the two modes within department-years. The hypothesis came from an exploratory analysis of 2010-2025; 2000-2009, unused by it, serves as a replication. In 17 departments outside the South-West, each degree-day above 32 C in July-August is associated with a 1.6% higher irrigated-to-rainfed yield ratio (one-sided wild cluster bootstrap by year, p = 0.007). This meets the study's decision rule; the sign is robust, its significance fragile: 62% of the sample comes from the exploratory years. The only new evidence, 2000-2009, keeps the sign at about half its 2010-2025 size, a difference driven by 2003 (p = 0.051, power about 8%), and fails with department-wide heat. Without the six departments with uplands it is not significant (p = 0.152), nor with a cluster jackknife against a Student distribution (p = 0.074; bootstrapped, p = 0.019). It holds with E-OBS temperatures, agrees with satellite surface temperature (which also reflects crop water stress), and replicates on silage maize, whose statistics partly copy grain's. With the vapour pressure deficit in the model, the association moves to the drying power of the air: it concerns hot, dry summers, not extreme heat as such. Repeated irrigated-rainfed ratios in the older statistics suggest imputation, a live threat; computed rainfed yields could bias it upwards. A planned interaction with water restrictions has the wrong sign. The result is an association, not the causal effect of irrigation. Here, department-clustered or Conley (200 km) standard errors reject a true null in 15% to 58% of placebo simulations. Wheat over 1959-2025 and out-of-sample forecasts give null results.
